Meal prep for Hyrox: 3 days that cover your whole week

4 min read · HybridFuelCo

You don't need seven different meals a day. Variety is overrated — fixed patterns are what people actually stick to. For a busy hybrid athlete training for Hyrox, three well-built days cover your whole week, hit your macros, and take one session to prep.

Why three day-templates, not seven meals

Every decision you remove from your week is one less thing to crack on. When your evenings are already split between training, work and sleep, deciding what to eat is friction you don't need. Three fixed day-templates give enough variety to not go mad, and few enough to prep in one Sunday session. You just rotate them: day A, B, C, A, B, C, A. Done.

This is also how real consistency happens. The athletes who hold their body composition and performance year-round rarely eat the most interesting food — they eat the same reliable meals often enough that hitting their protein and calories stops being a daily negotiation. Meal prep turns nutrition from a willpower problem into a logistics one, and logistics you can solve on a Sunday.

The perfect meal you don't make always loses to the simple one you do.

What to batch-cook

  • Protein: cook two sources in bulk (say chicken and beef, or tofu and eggs). That's your base for every day and the macro that matters most for recovery — aim for a palm-to-two-palms portion at each meal.
  • Carbs: rice, potato or oats — easy to scale up and keep well. These are your training fuel, so cook plenty.
  • Veg: roast one big tray at once; scoop a portion each day for volume, fibre and micronutrients.
  • Extras: a sauce, some fruit and a bag of nuts round the week out without adding cooking time.

Weigh your portions when you pack, not when you eat — then your day adds up without another thought. A kitchen scale and a set of containers do more for your results than any supplement.

A worked example week

Here's how three templates can look for someone around 2500 kcal and 180 g protein a day:

  • Day A: oats, yoghurt and berries; chicken, rice and roast veg; salmon, potato and salad.
  • Day B: eggs and wholegrain toast; beef, potato and veg; tofu or white fish, rice and greens.
  • Day C: a protein smoothie and fruit; turkey wrap with salad; a steak or bean bowl with sweet potato.

Rotate those three and your week is built. The exact grams per meal are what the HybridFuelCo app fills in, along with a shopping list that matches the plan — so your prep session has a clear target instead of guesswork.

The training-day versus rest-day twist

Your three templates stay the same; only your carbs shift. On a hard training day — a long run, a heavy lift or a Hyrox conditioning session — you scoop a bigger portion of rice or potato; on a rest day a smaller one, keeping protein steady. That keeps your weekly average on target while you train with a full tank. It's the same idea we cover in eating around training, applied to a batch-cooked kitchen.

Storage and food safety — the chef's part

Prep is only useful if the food is still good on Thursday. Cool cooked food quickly and get it in the fridge within a couple of hours; most cooked proteins and grains keep three to four days refrigerated. Anything beyond that, freeze in single portions and pull them out the night before. Keep sauces and anything crunchy separate so texture survives, and reheat rice and chicken until piping hot rather than lukewarm. These small habits are the difference between a week of prep you actually eat and a fridge of containers you quietly bin.

Chef's tricks so you actually eat it

  • Season generously and switch flavour profile by day (Mediterranean, Asian, Mexican) — same base, different feel.
  • Keep a good sauce or dressing on the side; it turns dull prep into something you look forward to.
  • Mind texture: something crunchy (nuts, seeds, roasted veg) keeps every box interesting.
Important. General nutrition information, not medical advice. Adjust portions to your goals and how you feel; when in doubt or with a medical condition, consult a doctor or dietitian.
